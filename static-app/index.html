What Is a Live Police Scanner?

A police scanner is a radio receiver that can tune in to the frequencies used by the police, fire departments and ambulance services. The name comes from the way the receiver works: it scans through a list of channels, stopping whenever it finds activity, so you hear the calls as they happen.

A live scanner feed is the same idea, delivered over the internet. A receiver in the county picks up the radio traffic and streams it online, so you can listen on a phone or a computer without owning a radio at all. That is the easiest way to start, because there is nothing to buy and nothing to program. You simply open the feed for the county you want to hear and listen. If you enjoy it, you can move on to a scanner app or a receiver of your own, but the live feed is a good way to find out whether the hobby is for you.

How Scanner Radio Works

Public safety radio in the United States uses a few different kinds of system, and it helps to know which one you are listening to. The oldest kind is the conventional channel, where one frequency carries one channel. A sheriff's dispatch channel, for example, is one frequency, and anyone with a basic scanner can receive it.

The second kind is the trunked system. On a trunked system, a group of frequencies is shared between many users, and a central controller assigns a frequency for each transmission. The best-known trunked system in Alabama is the statewide Alabama Interoperable Radio System, known as AIRS, which is a P25 system, and many counties also use it alongside their conventional channels. A trunked system is more efficient, because it lets many agencies share a small number of frequencies, but it needs a trunk-tracking scanner to follow, because the conversation moves from one frequency to another. A P25 Phase II trunk-tracking scanner is the standard choice for this kind of system, while a Phase I-only scanner cannot decode P25 Phase II traffic.

The third kind of system you will come across is a digital system such as NXDN, which is used by some city networks. Like a trunked system, it needs a receiver that can decode it. The practical point is the same in every case: before you buy a receiver, check which systems the county you want to hear actually uses, and make sure the receiver you choose can decode them. As one example of how talkgroups are listed, AIRS lists talkgroups including 20001 for sheriff dispatch, 21190 to 21194 for fire and EMS dispatch, 23402 for police dispatch and 28111 for the state highway patrol, which shows how much of the traffic in a busy county runs through the trunked system.

What You Can Hear

The agencies you can hear on a live scanner include the sheriff's office, which covers the county as a whole, the police and fire departments in the county's towns, the county's volunteer fire departments, the ambulance service, and the state highway patrol, which covers the main roads.

Fire and EMS traffic is usually the easiest to follow, because the calls are described in plain language: an address, the nature of the call, and which units are being sent. Law enforcement traffic is shorter and more clipped, with officers giving locations and short status updates, and it takes a little longer to get used to. After a few sessions you will know which channels carry which kind of traffic, and you will be able to follow an incident from the first call through to the units arriving on scene.

How to Listen

The simplest way to start is with the live audio feed for the county, which lets you hear what is on the air right now without any equipment at all. From there, you can move on to a scanner app on your phone, or to a receiver of your own. Start with one or two channels rather than trying to scan everything at once, so you learn what each one sounds like. Listen at different times of day, because the pattern of traffic changes: mornings are often quiet, weekday afternoons are busier, and evenings bring a mix of patrol and fire calls.

A live feed can drop out from time to time, because the audio comes from third-party providers and feed availability can change, so a quiet feed does not always mean a quiet county. The same is true of a receiver: a quiet channel may simply mean that nothing is happening on it at that moment, or that the agency has moved to another channel. Patience is part of the hobby, and the quiet stretches make the busy ones more interesting when they come.

Listening Etiquette

A scanner is a way to understand what is happening in your county, not a way to get involved in it. Never transmit on public-safety frequencies, and never use scanner traffic to interfere with police, fire or EMS work. Do not drive to the scene of an incident you have heard about. If you hear something that worries you, do not act on it yourself: call the emergency services and let them deal with it.

It is also worth remembering that the people you hear on a scanner are doing a difficult job, often in stressful circumstances, and the names and addresses in a call belong to real people having a bad day. Treat what you hear with respect, and listen to learn about your county, leaving the responding to the people whose job it is.

What Is Clear and What Is Encrypted

Not every channel can be heard. Some agencies broadcast in the clear, which means anyone with a suitable receiver can listen, while other channels are encrypted, which means the audio cannot be decoded by a listener. Fire and EMS channels are usually in the clear, and they are the best place to start, because the traffic is easy to follow. Law enforcement channels are more likely to be encrypted, and where they are, there is no way around it.

In Alabama, listening to traffic broadcast in the clear is lawful for personal use, but knowingly intercepting an encrypted public-safety transmission is a Class C felony under Alabama Code 13A-10-16, and federal law draws the same line. Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a radio to listen to a live scanner?

No. A live feed plays in a browser or an app, so you can listen on a phone or a computer with no equipment at all. A receiver is only needed if you want to listen directly to the radio signals yourself.

Why can I hear fire and EMS but not the police?

Fire and EMS channels are usually broadcast in the clear, while many police channels are encrypted. An encrypted channel cannot be decoded by any listener, and that is by design.

Is it legal to listen to a police scanner?

In Alabama, listening to traffic broadcast in the clear is lawful for personal use. Knowingly intercepting an encrypted transmission is a Class C felony under Alabama Code 13A-10-16, and you must never transmit on public-safety frequencies.

Why is the feed quiet or dropping out?

A live feed can drop out because the audio comes from third-party providers and feed availability can change, so a quiet feed does not always mean a quiet county. Try another county nearby, or come back later in the day.
